UC Berkeley Center for Teaching & Learning

Turning AI feedback into research people can use

Students and educators are already making decisions about AI. They need evidence about when it helps learning, what support it provides, and how instructors can integrate it responsibly.

October 2026 – present Current

Problem

AI and other learning technologies are moving into classrooms faster than many professors and students can evaluate them. Instructors need evidence about whether students should use these tools, how much use is appropriate, and when AI-generated grading or feedback improves learning versus distracting from it.

A separate retention problem runs through the course research: students consider a class, enroll, and sometimes drop without instructors having a clear view of which expectations, course experiences, or support gaps drove that decision. My work spans AI use across courses, feedback in an advanced physics lab, and enrollment and retention in Korean language courses.

My role is to turn student and educator feedback into findings people can use without overstating work that is still in progress.

What I do

I clean and analyze survey data with Python, pandas, and Google Colab; code qualitative feedback into themes; compare those themes with quantitative patterns; and write recommendations for educators.

01

AI FAST and AI use across courses

I clean, filter, and analyze survey responses to understand how students use AI in different coursework, what they find helpful, and where they need guidance. The workflow is responses → coded themes and quantitative patterns → recommendations for educators. The public Berkeley page describes related mixed-methods research on GenAI, critical thinking, and information literacy. I have not named a government reporting recipient because that detail is not yet confirmed.

02

Physics 111B AI lab grader

I support research with Professor Eric Y. Ma on AI-generated grading and feedback for advanced physics lab reports. Student and GSI focus groups ask about tone, formatting, usefulness, alignment with human grading, and possible bias. These are research questions, not published findings.

03

Korean course enrollment and retention

I support focus-group research on why students consider Korean, why some drop the class, and what changes might improve the experience. We investigate motivation and expectations before enrollment, the learning environment after the course starts, and the factors behind continuing or dropping.

What changed and what I’m learning

Across the three projects, the useful output is not a pile of comments. It is a traceable path from a research question to evidence, themes, and a recommendation—with private surveys, transcripts, and internal reports kept off the public site.

Next steps

I am continuing analysis and focus-group work. Findings will be added only when the project teams approve a public breakdown and confirm which work is complete.
