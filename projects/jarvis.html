Jarvis

Student teams make decisions live. Jarvis lets a teammate who can’t join still weigh in while it matters.

My role
Product and user testing; design and demo
Format

Problem

Meetings lose momentum when someone has to leave the call to search another tab, type every decision into notes, or reconstruct what the group was discussing. Important pivots and open questions are easy to forget while the team is focused on talking.

The problem becomes larger when someone cannot attend. People inside the meeting have to keep switching to messaging apps to update them, while a transcript gives the absent teammate many words but not always a clear overview of what changed, what was decided, or where their input is still useful. We built Jarvis as a live AI participant that can answer aloud, track the meeting, and let an absent teammate participate through Slack while the decision is still open.

What I did

My confirmed contributions are product and user testing and design and demo. The team built the system together; I have not claimed ownership of a more specific interaction until one decision or flow is confirmed.

Why it mattered

Every late joiner costs the group a few minutes of catching up, and every missing teammate is a voice that isn’t in the decision. We wanted a teammate that keeps people in the conversation whether they’re in the room or not.

What we chose to build

  • Spoken answers in Meet

    Say “Hey Jarvis” and ask a question. Jarvis answers out loud in a few sentences and posts the source on the board.

  • A live decision board

    Jarvis shares a whiteboard that fills in decided, open, and to-do notes for each agenda topic as the team talks.

  • Two-way Slack participation

    An absent teammate can ask for a recap, send a message into the meeting, and get the team’s reply back in Slack.

One exchange, from Slack to the meeting and back

  1. 01

    Ask from Slack

    A teammate who couldn’t join asks Jarvis for a rundown, then types a message for the group: they know someone at a possible sponsor.

  2. 02

    Raised in the meeting

    Jarvis raises its hand on the board instead of interrupting. When someone says “Go ahead, Jarvis,” it reads the message aloud.

  3. 03

    Reply back in Slack

    The team answers out loud, and Jarvis sends that reply back to the teammate in Slack.

What changed and what I learned

What shipped

A working end-to-end flow at the hackathon: Jarvis joins from a Meet link, answers by voice, keeps the board, relays both ways with Slack, and posts notes and proposed memory updates after the call.

What we measured

In our test calls, the hand went up about 1.5 seconds after a Slack message arrived. Answers that needed no tools took about 2 seconds, and web-backed answers took 8 to 11 seconds.

What we learned

Voice changes the design problem: latency and who is allowed to speak matter as much as answer quality. Sheng Rui Leom’s public team post records demo requests for automatic joining, chat input, and screen-share awareness; these are feedback and future ideas, not shipped features.

Three weeks later

A related industry signal

Three weeks after our team presented Jarvis at Cal Hacks 13.0, Anthropic published the linked post. The timing stood out because we had just spent the hackathon exploring how an AI teammate could move context between the tools where a team already works.

Jarvis was our own hackathon prototype: it joined Google Meet, kept a live record of decisions and open questions, carried an absent teammate’s message in from Slack, returned the group’s spoken reply, and proposed team-memory updates for a person to review. I’m treating Anthropic’s post as related context—not as a claim that the products are identical, or that anyone copied anyone.

Next steps

The three public feedback directions are automatic joining, asking Jarvis through Meet chat, and screen-share awareness. My priority is intentionally left open until I can tie it to one specific user-testing observation or product decision.
