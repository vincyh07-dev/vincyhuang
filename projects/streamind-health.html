Case study · Software Engineer Intern

Streamind Health

Camera-based biofeedback on a phone: reading a pulse from the tiny color changes in a face.

Role
Software Engineer Intern
When
October 2025 – May 2026
Company
Streamind Health
Focus
rPPG signal extraction, iOS

Problem

Remote health sessions make it harder for a therapist to notice the physical signals they would normally pick up in person. A video call shows a face and voice, but it does not automatically give the therapist a simple physiological reading such as heart rate.

Streamind explores whether the patient’s camera can provide that extra signal during a session. The technical challenge is that lighting, motion, device differences, skin tone, and other visible skin conditions can overwhelm the tiny pulse-related color changes in facial video. The product already worked on laptops; my work moved capture and visualization toward iOS.

What I did

I worked on how the app gets a pulse signal out of camera video and on bringing that workflow from laptop to iOS.

  • Facial region selection. I tested different shapes and combinations of forehead and cheek regions, placed using facial landmarks.
  • RGB signal extraction. I tracked average RGB values over time and tested CHROM, a chrominance-based method that combines color-channel information to help isolate pulse-related changes.
  • Adapting for mobile. I helped adapt the workflow for phones, evaluated camera performance and signal-quality tradeoffs, and built real-time data capture and visualization.

Why the signal is hard to get

The pulse is a very small change hidden in noise. Head movement, talking, room light, automatic exposure, device cameras, skin tone, and skin conditions such as acne can all affect the signal. Reliability across these conditions remains an evaluation question rather than a claim.

From camera to heart rate

  1. 01

    Camera frames

    The phone’s front camera records the face, frame after frame. Each heartbeat changes skin color very slightly, far too little to see.

  2. 02

    Facial regions

    Facial landmarks locate the forehead and cheeks in every frame, so we keep sampling the same patches of skin as the head moves.

  3. 03

    Color signal

    Averaging the RGB values inside each region, frame by frame, turns video into a color signal. The pulse is a small ripple inside it, mixed with motion and lighting changes.

  4. 04

    Heart-rate estimate

    CHROM combines color-channel information to help isolate pulse-related changes. Band-pass filtering keeps plausible heart-rate frequencies, and frequency analysis picks the strongest rhythm as the beats-per-minute estimate.

A tradeoff: where to sample

Every sampling region is a compromise. A larger region averages more pixels, which smooths out camera noise, but it is more likely to catch hair, eyebrows, or shadow, which carry no pulse. The forehead is broad and fairly flat but is often partly covered by hair. The cheeks show color change well but move when someone talks or smiles. Shape matters too: a region tied to the landmarks keeps sampling the same skin as the head turns, while a fixed box drifts.

I compared forehead and cheek regions and region shapes as part of testing. The current draft does not claim that one region won because the exact comparison conditions and result still need confirmation.

What we chose to build

On mobile, the heart-rate monitor runs from the front camera, alongside history, guided breathing, and mood tabs. The team also built a webcam prototype that runs the same idea in a browser.

Next steps

I would stratify testing by device, lighting, motion, skin tone, and visible skin conditions such as acne, then choose regions frame by frame using a signal-quality score. I would also give users immediate guidance when lighting or motion makes a reading unreliable.
