Debugging MRI models and labels

I build a prostate MRI image-quality classifier and test liver-segmentation models, using visual review to find whether an error comes from the image, the mask, or the model.

May 2026 – present Current

Problem

When an MRI scan is too unclear for diagnosis, the problem may not be discovered until a radiologist reviews it later. That delay can mean contacting the patient, rescheduling, and asking them to return for another scan—an inefficient and stressful outcome for both patients and clinical teams.

Earlier automated quality checks could help flag unusable images sooner. In the segmentation work, a second issue appeared: two different questions were getting mixed together—is the original MRI usable, and does a predicted mask line up with the anatomy? A single score could not explain which part had failed.

What I did

Prostate MRI image-quality classification

I built and trained a PyTorch pipeline to classify diagnostic image quality across more than 5,000 prostate MRI scans, reaching 92% accuracy.

Image-quality classification

Liver segmentation

  1. I began with a U-Net baseline. Performance was lower than expected, and overlay review showed that I needed a clearer rubric.
  2. I defined Good as a usable original image whose mask lines up, Bad as a mask in the wrong location, and Uncertain as an image with no predicted mask.
  3. I labeled more examples and moved to nnU-Net. In my tests, runs were faster and segmentations looked better; exact gains are withheld until the runs are verified.
  4. The additional labels support error analysis and the next round of training instead of treating every low score as the same failure.

PyTorch · U-Net · nnU-Net · visual overlay review

What changed and what I learned

The rubric separates image quality from mask placement. Dice measures overlap between a predicted mask and a reference mask: 1 is complete overlap and 0 is none. The segmentation pipeline achieved a 0.89 mean Dice score, while visual review helped explain the different ways a prediction could fail.

Next steps

I would split misplaced masks from missing masks, then test each group separately. Misplaced masks point toward localization errors; missing masks point toward detection or pipeline failures. That makes the next experiment more specific than simply adding more data.
